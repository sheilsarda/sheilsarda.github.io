# Selling a robot you haven't finished building

### Notes on the sales motion at a grocery automation startup, and the simulation stack standing behind every number in the contract

A grocery automation company has to sell a system before it is done. The mechanical design is frozen, the robots move, the demo runs. But the throughput number in the contract is a number the production system has never actually hit, because the production system does not fully exist yet. You are quoting a future state and committing to it in writing.

This is the central tension of go-to-market at an early-stage robotics company. Two mechanisms make it survivable. The first is a clear ownership model for the sales motion, so the right person is on the hook at each stage. The second is a simulation stack that tells you, before you sign, what you can credibly put in the contract. The second one is the more important. The sim is not a side project that engineering runs for fun. It is the instrument that decides the SLA.

I joined Fulfil after the latest generation of robots were mechanically complete. Fulfil is a Series B grocery automation company, founded in 2020, around a hundred people, headquartered in Mountain View. The product is five different robot types that work together to fulfil online grocery orders, deployed for clients including Kroger and Amazon Whole Foods. I came in on the factory controls team, the group responsible for making the machines move and turning a building full of hardware into a running fulfilment center. This post is about the two things I watched most closely from that seat: how the company sold systems that were still being built, and how the simulation infrastructure grew up to support those sales.

## Who owns the sale, and when

The go-to-market motion was split across three roles. The Director of Business Development ran the commercial relationship. The Chief Systems Architect owned the technical feasibility of anything that deviated from the standard product. The VP of Engineering, which I use here as shorthand for the mechanical, electrical, and software teams that take over a program once it leaves prototyping, owned delivery. The interesting part is not the roles. It is how accountability moves between them as a deal matures.

Early on, the system does not need to be production ready. It needs to be demo ready. Videos and live demos stand in for everything the simulation stack would otherwise have to prove. The pitch passes the sniff test if it survives the three questions every prospective client asks: what does a unit cost and how does that scale as the footprint grows, what fulfilment metrics can the system actually hit versus the human alternative, and what backend integration work is required to make it run inside their facility. At this stage Business Development is both responsible and accountable. The Systems Architect supports, the engineering org is consulted, and that is it.

Contract negotiation is where the weight shifts. The metrics in the agreement are concrete and unforgiving: throughput, mean time between interruptions, the dial-up timeline before the system is fully operational, and intermediate stage gates for hardware and software. Signing up for those numbers requires confidence that the numbers are real. That confidence comes from simulation, which is why this stage is the hinge of the entire motion. Engineering becomes accountable here, sharing responsibility with Business Development, because engineering is the group that has to believe the SLA before the company commits to it.

Post-sales delivery shifts again. Engineering is responsible for shipping a working system, but accountability lands on the Systems Architect, because by now the program may include hardware or behavior that was not in the original out-of-the-box solution, and that is the architect's domain. The single useful way to see all three stages is to track where the R and the A sit as the deal ages:

| Stage | Responsible | Accountable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pitch and demo | Business Dev | Business Dev |
| Contract negotiation | Engineering, Business Dev | Engineering |
| Post-sales delivery | Engineering | Systems Architect |

The distinction between the Systems Architect and the engineering org is worth stating plainly, because it explains the handoff. The architect owns new hardware programs and any feasibility question where a client wants something slightly different from the standard system. They decide buy versus build for each module, they spec the mechanisms, actuators, and compute nodes, and they own the machine while it is still an idea. Engineering owns it once it is a product. The accountability migrating to the architect post-sales is the company admitting that a delivered system is rarely the catalog system.

## Simulation as the thing that lets you sign

When I arrived, the simulation infrastructure was young. Compute lived on-prem, and we spun up nodes in Kubernetes for the services a factory needs: cloud software, centralized planning, data ingestion. The highest fidelity we could test at was a 2D robot dashboard built on Phaser, a web game framework, where each robot moves around in pixel space. It ran on three simplifying assumptions. Ideal kinematics govern position at every instant. Robots can phase through each other. Plans execute nominally a hundred percent of the time.

That sounds primitive, and for collision behavior or contact it was useless. But it was built to be timing accurate, and timing accuracy was the whole point. We spent real effort measuring how long motion primitives took on actual hardware, single-axis moves, multi-axis moves, picks, places, and then tuned the sim so the thread sleep times for each commanded action landed within five percent of the real world. A timing-accurate 2D sim cannot tell you whether two robots will collide. It can tell you, with confidence, what throughput a facility of a given layout will produce. We trusted those numbers to transfer to hardware within plus or minus ten percent. That ten percent is the margin a Business Development lead is implicitly betting on when they sign a throughput SLA. The sim is upstream of the contract.

Timing accuracy was a standing tax, not a one-time build. Every time the firmware or controls team made a primitive faster, by unlocking parallelism in a sequence or pushing the motor controls harder, the sim drifted out of calibration until someone updated it. That feedback loop had a lag, and the lag is a real risk. A sim that is stale in the optimistic direction tells you that you can promise more than you can deliver.

The 2D sim had a hard ceiling. It could not test anything that changed how the machines were commanded, because it modeled outcomes, not mechanics. Any change to the command layer got tested on hardware for the first time in our R&D facility, with no software safety net. An intern once tried to get an Unreal Engine factory sim running to close that gap. It died on a practical wall. Our planning codebase was in C#, Unreal did not play well with C#, and bridging them meant a rewrite large enough that nobody could justify it. Unreal would have bought us 3D, but 3D was not a box we needed checked. We had to validate machine code on real hardware regardless, and on hardware you can just watch it run. Skipping that rewrite was the right call, and it is a clean example of an engineering philosophy I came to trust: if a project does not improve the end-to-end result, the time and money belong somewhere else.

The instinct in robotics is that a better simulator is a more physically realistic one. That is the world-models position, and it is the bet behind a lot of current funding: photoreal rendering, soft-body contact, differentiable physics, the works. For policy learning that bet is reasonable. For a sim whose job is to gate a contract, it is the wrong axis. A throughput SLA does not care whether the render is photoreal. It cares whether the timing transfers, and our crude 2D model transferred timing to within ten percent while a beautiful 3D physics sim would have told us nothing new about throughput and cost us a codebase rewrite to get. Fidelity is not one number. The fidelity that mattered for selling was temporal, and the temporal axis is the one the impressive sims tend to underinvest in.

Landing a client is what forced the next jump. Real contracts carried features that demanded a physics-real sim, so we built a hardware abstraction layer that let us swap a simulator in where real hardware would normally sit, with no change to the code above it. Two tools earned their place. MuJoCo started as a machine-specific tool, useful for asking whether a new control policy on one subsystem reduced jerk relative to the status quo. The more valuable use came from extrapolating it. We modeled a scoped-down factory, some number of aisles across some number of zones running in parallel, and measured cumulative throughput. There are cheaper ways to estimate aggregate throughput. The reason to do it in physics is the lesson it teaches when you go from one robot to many: you find out whether speeding up a given subsystem actually moves the end-to-end number, or whether the bottleneck lives somewhere else entirely. That is the same philosophy as the Unreal decision, now pointed inward at our own roadmap. A faster picker that does not move the line is a faster picker nobody should fund. Isaac Sim came in for a narrower job, fine-tuning VLA policies for the manipulation tasks we wanted to pull off the manual side of the fulfilment system.

## Where the next sim stack gets built

The shape of this story is a sim stack chasing the contract. Timing-accurate 2D was enough while the thing we sold was throughput on a fixed set of behaviors. Physics-real MuJoCo and Isaac Sim showed up the moment we sold features that changed how the machines act, and the moment manipulation became a contractable capability rather than a research demo. The sales motion and the sim stack are not two stories. The RACI handoff describes who is accountable for the SLA at each stage, and the sim describes what they are allowed to put in it. Move the sim and you move the contract.

The piece nobody has shipped is the merge. A 2D model that is timing accurate but mechanically blind, and a 3D physics sim that is mechanically rich but never calibrated for timing, are two halves of one tool that does not exist yet at full factory scale. The hard part is not building either half. It is keeping a physics-real, contact-aware sim calibrated to within ten percent on timing while the firmware team keeps making the primitives faster underneath it. That calibration loop, the standing tax, is what breaks first when you scale fidelity.

So here is the falsifiable part. As VLA-driven manipulation moves from R&D into the SLA, the gating sim for grocery automation will stop being a 2D timing model and become a physics sim that is also timing accurate, because you will need both the contact realism and the throughput number from a single source of truth. The companies that win the next wave of grocery and warehouse contracts over the next eighteen to twenty-four months will be the ones whose physics sims hit timing accuracy, not just visual or contact fidelity. If a competitor signs aggressive manipulation-throughput SLAs in that window on the back of a sim that is physics-real but not timing accurate, watch for them to miss their dial-up timelines. The sim that cannot tell you when, only whether, is the sim that gets you sued.
