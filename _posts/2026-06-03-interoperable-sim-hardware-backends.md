## Glossary

**On-device planner.** The component that decides what to do next. It issues goals and consumes state. It runs the same whether a simulator or a real robot sits behind it. The whole post is about keeping it that way.

**Backend.** Whatever the planner talks to. Either a physics simulator or the real robot's hardware driver. The design goal is that the planner cannot tell which.

**Sim bridge.** The layer that makes a simulator present itself to the planner exactly as the hardware driver would. A correct bridge is invisible.

**Hardware driver.** The software that talks to the real arm and sensors and exposes them to the rest of the stack. In ROS this is typically a `ros2_control` `SystemInterface`.

**`ros2_control` SystemInterface.** The standard contract a backend implements. It accepts commands and reports state through a fixed set of interfaces. A simulator that implements the same interface is plug-and-play against the same planner.

**TF tree.** The bookkeeping that converts data between coordinate frames (camera, arm base, end effector, world). Each frame has one parent and stores its pose relative to that parent. Static edges are bolted-on geometry measured once by calibration. Dynamic edges are forward kinematics of the moving joints, recomputed every cycle. Transforms are timestamped and looked up at a time, with interpolation between the nearest stamps.

**Sim time vs wall time.** Simulators publish their own clock on `/clock`, and nodes that set `use_sim_time` follow it instead of the system clock. This lets the sim run faster or slower than real time, or pause, while every timestamp stays consistent.

**Lockstep vs free-running.** In lockstep the backend advances only when the planner has consumed the last state, which makes replay deterministic. Free-running lets the backend advance on its own clock, which is closer to reality but non-deterministic.

**sim2real gap.** The residual difference between simulator and reality on a given channel, once the interface matches and the timing is honest. It is what is left over after the easy mismatches are removed.

**Domain randomization.** Training or testing across a spread of physical parameters so the deployment case is likely inside the range seen. It averages over a known range. It does not detect when reality falls outside that range.

## The swap test

Put an on-device planner in front of a robotic arm and give it one job: pick an object off a table and place it somewhere else. The planner issues a goal, the arm moves, and state flows back. Joint angles, the end-effector pose, the gripper's open width, the force on contact, the depth image of the table. The planner decides the next move from that state. This is the loop, and it does not care in principle whether the thing executing the goal is a real arm or a physics simulator pretending to be one.

That indifference is the entire design target. A correct sim bridge makes the simulator and the real arm interchangeable backends. Swap one for the other and the planner does not change a line. I will call this the swap test, and it is a stronger requirement than it sounds. It is not enough that both backends accept a goal and report a pose. The planner must be unable to tell which one it is talking to from anything it observes. The moment the planner can distinguish them, the bridge is leaking, and the leak is always one of three things.

It is leaking through the interface, meaning the two backends do not present the same messages. It is leaking through timing, meaning the messages arrive at different rates or with different latency, or one backend's clock is dishonest. Or it is leaking through fidelity, meaning the content of the messages is true on one side and false on the other. What crosses the boundary, when it crosses, and whether it is true. Those three partition the failure space, and the rest of this post is one section each.

One scope line before the sections, because it saves trouble later. The swap test governs faithfulness, not safety. A bridge can pass every part of it and the robot can still be unsafe, because indistinguishability says the simulator matches reality, not that reality is acceptable. Safety is a fourth concern, orthogonal to the three contracts, and the mobile-robot case at the end is where it forces its way in. I have spent enough time on SIL and HIL rigs at Fulfil to know that the bugs which survive longest are the ones where the simulator was faithful and the system was wrong anyway.

## Contract 1: interface, what crosses the boundary

Start with the messages, because most of the "is it plug and play" question is decided here. For pick-and-place the command channel going down to the backend carries three things: a target for the arm (either an end-effector pose or a joint trajectory), a gripper command, and the pick-and-place action itself with its goal, periodic feedback, and terminal result. The state channel coming back up carries joint states, the end-effector pose, the gripper state, contact or force readings, the perception streams, and the transform tree. That last one matters more than its line in the list suggests, and it gets its own treatment below.

Plug and play has a precise meaning in this picture. A simulator is interchangeable with the hardware exactly when it implements the same server interface the hardware driver implements. In a ROS 2 stack that interface is a `ros2_control` `SystemInterface`, and the design has already anticipated the swap. The same abstract interface is reimplemented by the real driver, by the Gazebo plugin, and by a mock. The mock components package exists so you can test the framework's plumbing without hardware access, exercising your controllers and message flow against a stand-in. Going from simulator to mock to real robot is, mechanically, swapping which plugin string the `<hardware>` tag loads. That is the swap test made concrete, and it is why "what hardware is the sim mocking" is an interface question first: the mock has to present the same interface as the specific arm and sensors it stands in for, or the swap fails the moment contact matters.

### The transform tree is an interface stream

A robot has many coordinate frames. The world origin, the arm's base, each link, the end-effector, the camera lens. Every measurement and every command lives in some frame. A depth point arrives measured from the camera. A grasp goal is meaningful in the arm's base frame. The TF tree is the bookkeeping that converts between any two of them, and it is a tree because each frame has exactly one parent, with the edge between them storing the child's pose relative to the parent. Ask for the camera relative to the base and TF walks the edges from one to the other, multiplying transforms along the way.

Two kinds of edges, and the split runs through everything below. Static transforms do not change. The camera is bolted to a mount some fixed distance and angle from the arm base, that geometry is measured once during calibration, and it is published once on a latched topic so any node that joins late still receives it. Dynamic transforms change every cycle. When the arm moves, the end-effector's pose relative to the base changes continuously, and that pose is not measured directly. It is computed: take the live joint angles, take the robot's kinematic model from the URDF, run forward kinematics, and you get the frame poses. So the TF tree is the URDF plus live joint states turned into frame poses over time. Static edges are bolted-on geometry. Dynamic edges are forward kinematics of the moving parts.

One more mechanic, because it bites in the off-node case. Every transform is timestamped, and you look transforms up at a time. TF keeps a short buffer of recent transforms and interpolates between the two nearest stamps. When a depth frame stamped at time T arrives, the planner asks TF for the camera-to-base transform at T, and TF interpolates the arm pose to that instant. That is how a point sensed mid-motion lands in the right place.

### Designing the planner to not care which robot it is

The TF tree hands you the rule for configuration-agnostic design directly. The planner should never hardcode where anything is. It should take sensor data, transform it into a canonical frame (the base or the world), plan there, and emit goals there. Do that and the entire physical configuration of the robot lives in exactly two places: the static transforms that say where things are mounted, and the URDF that says how the arm is shaped. Swap the robot, swap those two, and the planner is untouched. This is the TF version of the swap test, and it reduces to two habits.

Consume transformed data, not raw-frame data. The planner takes a depth point, asks TF to express it in the base frame, and reasons there. A different camera mount is then just a different static transform, and the same planner code absorbs it. Output task-space goals, not joint trajectories. If the planner says "put the end-effector at this pose in the base frame" and lets a per-robot layer solve which joint angles get there, then a different arm with different link lengths needs only its own URDF and its own inverse kinematics. The planner's goal is byte-for-byte identical. The instant the planner emits joint angles directly, it is welded to one arm's geometry and the swap test is lost.

### Worked example: the planner upgrades to decimated depth, the bag has raw

Here is the example that shows the contracts are a checklist, not three sealed boxes. Suppose the planner gets upgraded to consume decimated depth maps, the downsampled point clouds it now expects on its perception input. The recorded episode you want to replay against it predates the upgrade and contains only raw depth. The planner's input schema changed; the data did not.

The fix enters cleanly through the interface. Add an adapter node that subscribes to the raw depth the bag publishes and republishes decimated depth on the topic and type the upgraded planner expects. Same boundary, new representation crossing it. Nothing structural changes, which is the point. The framework predicted that a schema change is an interface event.

It does not stay in the interface lane, though, and the diagnostic is what forces the other two traps into the open. The decimation node is computation, and computation is latency, so timing is now in play. The decimated output has to carry the original stamps, or stamps offset by a latency that matches reality, or replay determinism breaks and the planner can tell sim from real by timing alone. And in reality the decimation runs somewhere specific, almost certainly on-device or colocated with the planner, so if you drop the node on a remote sim machine you have moved the computation into a different clock domain and the timing signature gives the game away. Fidelity is in play too, because decimation is lossy and algorithm-dependent: voxel-grid downsampling, uniform stride, and a nearest-neighbor prefilter produce three different maps from one raw frame. If the sim node's decimator does not match the decimator the real robot runs on-device, you have injected a fresh sim2real gap at the exact representation the planner now depends on. The discipline is that the sim node runs the same decimation code as the on-device path, not a clean-room reimplementation of it.

Because the bag is fixed, you precompute rather than decimate live on every replay. Run the decimation once as an offline pass over the raw bag and write a derived bag. Replay stays real-time and deterministic, the raw bag stays the source of truth, and when the decimator changes again you re-derive instead of re-recording. So the answer to "what does this change about the solution" is: nothing structural. A schema change enters through interface, and the diagnostic then asks whether it moved time (it did) and whether it stayed truthful (only if the algorithm matches).

### Worked example: overriding a recorded configuration

The recorded episode carries raw depth in the camera frame plus the `/tf` and `/tf_static` for the one configuration it was captured in. Two different questions hide inside "make the planner agnostic to configuration," and they have different answers.

If the planner was written the agnostic way above, replaying the bag just works. It transforms the bag's raw depth through the bag's TF into the base frame and never cared about the specific mount. Design-time agnosticism is fully achievable and is the real answer to the question.

Testing the planner against a genuinely different configuration using this bag is where you hit a wall, and it is worth being precise about where. You can override the bag's static transforms: strip `/tf_static` out of the bag on replay and publish your own with a different camera mount. There is a concrete gotcha here. Static transforms are latched, so if you let the bag's version through and also publish your own, TF sees two conflicting static transforms and you get garbage. Strip first, then publish. What that override buys you is a calibration-sensitivity test. Inject a slightly wrong extrinsic and check whether the grasp still succeeds, which is legitimate and useful because calibration drifts in the field.

What it does not buy you is a different-viewpoint test. The pixels in the bag were captured from where the camera physically sat. Pretend the camera was mounted elsewhere and the data is now geometrically inconsistent, because you have relabeled where the points are without changing what was seen. Move the real mount and you would see different occlusions, a different field of view, surfaces that were hidden and surfaces that were not. A recording cannot regenerate that. Only the simulator can, by re-rendering the scene from the new viewpoint against the URDF for the new configuration. The clean line: you can perturb the interpretation of existing data, but you cannot recover an observation that was never captured. That is exactly the boundary between "test it on the bag" and "you need the sim," and it sets up the last section.

## Contract 2: timing, when it crosses and whether time is honest

The interface contract decides what messages exist. The timing contract decides when they arrive and whether the clock behind them is honest, and it is where putting the simulator on another machine stops being free.

Each channel has a rate. The control loop runs at its frequency, perception at the sensor's frame rate, force at the sensor's much higher rate. The bridge has to honor all of them, and it has to honor the clock domain they live in. ROS 2 makes the clock explicit. On real hardware the client libraries use wall-clock time. In simulation you usually want simulated time so you can run accelerated, slowed, or stepped, and so a sensor's timestamp can correspond to the data it carries rather than to the wall clock. The simulator publishes its clock on `/clock`, and any node with `use_sim_time` set follows that clock instead of the system one. The catch is that such a node ignores wall-clock time entirely and returns time zero until it has received its first `/clock` message, which is its own small class of startup bug. The standing rule is that code must use the ROS time API rather than the language's native clock, or it will read the wrong clock in one of the two worlds.

The honest-latency problem is the timing contract's sharpest edge. If the mock backend returns feedback instantly while the real arm returns it some milliseconds later, the bridge looks honest and is not, and the planner can distinguish sim from real on timing signature alone. The decimation node from the last section is the same hazard wearing a different hat: drop it on the remote sim machine and you have relocated compute across a network hop into a different clock domain. To keep the swap honest, the node sits at the same point in the pipeline it occupies in reality, under the same latency budget.

Stamp discipline is where this goes wrong in practice, and the failure has a documented shape. When a node grabs the current ROS time from a fresh clock object instead of the node's own clock during bag replay, the replayed data gets tagged with wall-clock time rather than the simulated time on `/clock`, and the deserialized sensor stream desynchronizes from everything else in the bag. The fix is mundane (stamp from the node's clock, feed every publisher from a single simulation-time source) and the bug is common. It is the decimation-node stamp warning, found in the wild.

The off-node case forces a transport decision. ROS 2's default DDS discovery works well inside a LAN but struggles across WAN, NAT, and firewalls, and it floods the network with discovery traffic as the node count grows. Put the simulator behind Docker Desktop's NAT or on a separate WSL2 host and discovery is the first thing to break. The standard answer is a Zenoh bridge, with one bridge process on the robot side and one on the operating host. It tunnels the ROS 2 traffic over a protocol built for exactly this, and it cuts the discovery chatter dramatically (the Zenoh project reports reductions of up to 99.97% in discovery traffic between bridged nodes). The reason this belongs in the timing contract and not the interface contract is that the transport you choose changes the latency and jitter profile of every channel that crosses it, which is precisely what the swap test is measuring.

Timestep selection falls out of the same contract rather than being a knob you turn by feel. There are three clocks, not one. Physics dt is bounded by integrator stability, and for pick-and-place the contact stiffness sets the ceiling: stiff contact needs a small step or the solver goes unstable. Control dt matches the rate of the real controller you are standing in for. Sensor dt matches the real sensor's rate, and any derived or decimated stream has to carry stamps consistent with that rate. The point is that the timestep is a timing-contract decision dictated by the stiffest contact and the fastest loop you have to stay honest to, not a number chosen for convenience.

## Contract 3: fidelity, whether what is behind the boundary is true

Interface gets the messages to match. Timing gets them to arrive honestly. Fidelity is whether their contents are true, and it is the contract that is never fully satisfied.

Dynamics fidelity is mass, inertia, friction, the actuator model, and above all the contact model. For pick-and-place the contact model is load-bearing, because the difference between a successful grasp and a dropped object lives in friction and contact stiffness that simulators approximate. There is a clean illustration of how leaky this is: when a simulated drive uses a velocity command interface, the wheels jump to the requested speed instantly while a real chassis cannot, so the model produces slip and odometry error that the real system would not produce in the same way. Sensor fidelity is the noise on each stream. Modern simulators model this explicitly. Isaac Sim renders depth through stereoscopic disparity with a noise model, and its RTX lidar is real ray tracing with configurable azimuth and elevation error means and standard deviations, so a reflective or absorptive surface returns a sparse or noisy cloud the way the real sensor would.

Geometric fidelity is the one that hides. The TF tree is the encoded physical configuration of the robot, so if the simulator's URDF places the camera where the model says it sits but the real camera is mounted two centimeters off, every transformed point is two centimeters wrong. That is a sim2real gap with nothing to do with dynamics or sensor noise. It is pure geometry, it is the most common silent failure, and it ties straight back to the calibration-sensitivity test from the interface section.

### What to precompute, and why it makes the mock real-time

Mocking a depth camera or a lidar live, frame by frame, is expensive, and most of the expense is wasted because most of the scene does not move. The discipline is to bake the static scene once. It is more efficient to precompute a cheap-to-evaluate field over the fixed geometry than to compute it on the fly from a surface reconstruction every step, and the standard move is to raycast against a fixed mesh or a signed distance field built once from the static scene, cache the result, and re-render only the dynamic objects each step. Real systems do exactly this. One well-known SDF mapper computes the field separately for the empty room and for each object, which is the static-baked-plus-dynamic-overlay pattern stated plainly, and the practical recipes are concrete (one mapping system recommends 20cm voxels with grouped raycasting). The offline decimation pass from the interface section is the same principle applied to a recording: anything static or replay-fixed gets computed once, the raw data stays the source of truth, and the derived product gets regenerated when its algorithm changes. Precompute is what keeps a mocked sensor cheap enough to run in real time and what keeps episode replay deterministic.

### The gap that is left

The sim2real gap is what remains on a channel once the interface matches and the timing is honest: the residual distance between the simulator's contact model, sensor noise, and geometry and the real world's. The literature is consistent that this gap's roots are physics discrepancies (rigid-body dynamics, friction, contact forces, material properties) on one side and sensor and rendering discrepancies on the other, and that no single technique closes it. Domain randomization is the workhorse mitigation, and it is worth saying clearly what it does. It varies simulator parameters across a distribution at training time so the policy is robust across that range. One representative manipulation setup randomizes seventeen dynamics parameters (box mass, surface friction, joint damping and friction, center of mass) to get a single box-pushing policy to transfer. That is averaging over a known range. It is not detecting that the deployed object falls outside the range, which is a different and harder problem.

The viewpoint point from the interface section is the cleanest statement of what fidelity ultimately owes you. Re-rendering the scene from a new viewpoint against the URDF for a new configuration is the thing the simulator can do that no recording ever can. Configuration-agnostic design is a discipline you apply at build time. Configuration-agnostic testing eventually forces you off bags and onto the simulator, and it does so precisely at the line between relabeling existing data and generating data that was never observed.

## A harder case: a wheeled robot among humans

Everything above assumed a passive backend. A depth camera reports state and has no intentions. An arm has dynamics but no agenda. Swap the arm for a wheeled robot with a pan/tilt head, give it a prior map of the building, and tell it to coexist with people, and the framework holds for most of the system and then strains in one specific and instructive way.

First, what this robot is. A prior map means localization, not mapping. The robot does not know exactly where it is on the map, because wheel odometry drifts, so it corrects the odometry guess against what the lidar sees. A lidar returns the outline of the surroundings, and a scan matcher slides and rotates that live outline over the prior map until it lines up best, which is an optimization: try a pose, measure how far each lidar point lands from the nearest mapped wall, sum the error, nudge the pose to reduce it, repeat. The filter that combines the two sources runs two steps forever. Predict: shift the estimate by the odometry and grow the uncertainty, because motion adds error. Correct: pull the estimate toward the lidar match and shrink the uncertainty, weighted by how much you trust the match. Nav2's AMCL does this with a particle cloud rather than a single estimate, scattering weighted pose hypotheses, scoring each by how well the live scan would match from there, and resampling around the winners. It is adaptive because it grows the particle count when the robot is uncertain and shrinks it when the pose has converged. The output that matters downstream is the transform from map to odometry, which is what lets every other component reason in map coordinates.

The pan/tilt head is a second control loop, and it exists because a two-degree-of-freedom head on a moving base cannot watch everywhere at once. Where to look is a decision, which turns out to be the load-bearing detail.

### How the two planners stay in sync

There are two planners at different ranges and rates. The global planner draws the long route across the map when the goal is set, occasionally, thinking about the whole building. Its output is pure geometry: an ordered list of poses from start to goal, a string laid on the floor, with no speeds and no schedule, spaced at the planner's search resolution and then smoothed. The local planner runs many times a second and thinks only about the next couple of meters, and its output is a velocity command, two numbers for a differential-drive base, sent to the wheels this cycle. The two speak different languages, and the sync between them is the interesting part.

The connection is a look-ahead point, the carrot. Each cycle the local planner takes the robot's current pose, finds the nearest point on the global path (the path is pruned to that nearest point so it never rescans the whole thing), looks a fixed distance further along, and aims the robot at that point while avoiding live obstacles. As the robot advances, the nearest point advances, the carrot slides forward, and the robot is pulled along the string. This is regulated pure pursuit in Nav2, and the look-ahead distance scales with speed: shorter when slow or in tight turns so the robot tracks the bend, longer on straights so it does not wobble. The carrot rarely lands exactly on a stored pose, so the planner interpolates position and heading between the two bracketing points. Global can be seconds stale and it does not matter, because it is only geometry; local is the loop that has to keep up with the world.

The handoff has one honest failure worth naming. Because the local planner aims at a look-ahead point and prunes the path to the nearest point, it can cut corners, shortcutting the planned route when the look-ahead is long. This is not hypothetical; it shows up as a real complaint where pruning to the closest point makes the controller skip parts of a deliberately planned path. The fixes are to shrink the look-ahead in tight spots or to penalize path deviation in the local planner's scoring, and naming it matters because it is the concrete crack in an otherwise clean decoupling.

### What happens when a human blocks the path

A person steps into the route. The lidar returns points where no wall should be, those points light up cells in the local costmap with a margin around them, and the local planner re-checks its options against that grid every cycle. If there is room, it picks a curving velocity that swings around the blocked cells toward the same carrot and rejoins the route. The global string is untouched. This is the common case and there is no drama. If the person fully blocks a narrow passage and no safe velocity makes progress, the local planner commands a stop and waits. If the block clears, the cells empty on the next scans and it resumes. If it persists past a timeout, the local planner gives up on the current route and kicks the problem to the global planner, which lays a new string around the now-known blockage, and the local planner starts chasing a carrot on the new string. If no route exists, the robot reports failure rather than nosing into the person.

The case with no clean fix is two agents mirroring each other. The robot eases left to pass, the person steps to their right to be polite, they re-block, both stop, both retry, and the loop repeats. The social-navigation literature has a name for it, the reciprocal dance, and the reason it happens is that the robot is reacting to where the person is, not negotiating where the person intends to go. Mitigations are a short randomized wait so the two stop mirroring, or simply stopping and letting the human resolve it. This is the moment "human as moving obstacle" stops being enough.

### Where the framework strains

The three contracts were built for a passive backend, and a human is not passive. A person is an agent whose behavior is a closed loop with the robot's behavior: the human avoids the robot, the robot avoids the human, and the two form a coupled system. That breaks a hidden assumption in the fidelity contract, which had quietly bundled two different things. Passive fidelity is sensor noise and dynamics, and it has a noise model you can write down and randomize over. Agent fidelity is the behavior of other actors, and it does not. Pretending it does is the trap.

The three ways to mock a human are not equivalent, and the difference is exactly passive versus agent fidelity. A scripted or recorded human is deterministic and replayable and completely non-reactive, which is good for regression on a fixed scenario and useless for evaluating the coupling. A reactive pedestrian model captures the coupling but buys it with an assumption: ORCA and the social-force model give collision avoidance only under reciprocity, the premise that every agent runs the same avoidance policy and shoulders its share. Real people violate that constantly, because they are distracted, or they expect right of way, or they freeze, and the assumption breaks down in exactly the socially unaware situations you most need to test. So the reactive model does not close the gap, it moves it from "no reaction" to "wrong reaction." The third option is adversarial, and the pan/tilt head plus the prior map make it possible. Since the map is static, you precompute per-cell visibility against it (a field-of-view-aware distance field is the established tool for this kind of occlusion-aware planning), so you know where an agent is invisible until close range. Then you spawn the worst-case human at those blind spots on purpose and test whether the head's look-policy clears them before the base commits to a path through. The static map is not just a planning input. It is the thing that lets you precompute where to put the dangerous human.

So the framework mostly holds. Interface and timing transfer without modification. Fidelity holds only after you split it into passive and agent fidelity. And there is one thing the swap test cannot see at all. A simulator can be perfectly indistinguishable from reality and the robot can still drive into someone, because indistinguishability is faithfulness, not safety. Coexisting with humans is an assurance question, governed for this class of machine by service-robot safety standards (ISO 13482 for personal-care robots, the speed-and-separation lineage from the collaborative-robot standards), and assurance is orthogonal to whether sim and real match. You can pass the swap test and still be unsafe. That is the scope line from the intro, paid off.

## Where the bridge earns its keep

The three contracts are not three boxes to check independently. They are a diagnostic you run in order, and the reason to run them in order is that a single change rarely stays in one. The decimated-depth upgrade was an interface change that moved time and threatened truth, and the only reason the fix was clean is that the diagnostic forced all three questions instead of letting "depth stuff" hide two of them. What crosses, when it crosses, whether it is true. Run them in that sequence on any bridge bug and the ripple shows itself.

What has not been built, as far as I can tell, is a bridge that certifies its own honest-latency and clock-domain properties rather than asserting them. Today the latency match between mock and hardware is a thing you believe because you wrote the bridge carefully, and you find out you were wrong in the field. A bridge that measured the per-channel latency and jitter of the real backend and held the simulator to the same distribution would turn indistinguishability from a claim into a test. That is the piece I would build first.

The prediction I will put a date on: by 2028 the off-node simulator becomes the default rather than the exception, and a transport bridge plus an honest-latency shim becomes standard tooling in the same way `ros2_control` made the hardware-swap standard. The passive contracts will get tooling because they have measurable targets. The agent-fidelity problem will still be open, because it is the one part of this that has no noise model to randomize over, and you cannot build a standard tool against a target you cannot write down. If in 2028 there is a widely used sim bridge that auto-verifies its timing contract and still no agreed way to validate a robot against the people it will share a floor with, this post called it. If both are solved, or neither, it did not.
